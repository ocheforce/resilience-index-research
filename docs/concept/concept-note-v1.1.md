The Resilience Index

A Low-Cost Composite Clinical-Cognitive Measure of Physiological and Cognitive Recovery Following Real-World Stress

Principal Investigator: Ali Wisdom (Penforce), Medical Laboratory Scientist and independent biomedical research analyst

Problem

Most health and fitness monitoring is reactive: problems are often identified after they have already affected health, function, or performance. Existing approaches also tend to focus on individual signals rather than the coordinated recovery of multiple physiological and cognitive domains.

In Nigeria, access to multidomain health monitoring remains concentrated in relatively well-resourced clinical and research settings. This creates an opportunity to investigate whether a low-cost, field-deployable approach can measure how an individual's physiological and cognitive state changes following a real-world stressor and how closely it returns toward baseline over time.

The proposed study addresses this gap by developing and testing a composite measure of recovery capacity using point-of-care biomarkers, wearable HRV, and brief cognitive assessments.

Research Gap

Existing monitoring approaches capture important but partial aspects of recovery. Consumer wearable platforms such as WHOOP and Oura provide extensive autonomic and cardiovascular recovery information, while many academic resilience and cognitive-risk approaches rely on self-report, specialized testing, or resource-intensive measurements.

We have not identified a validated, low-cost composite approach that integrates clinical biomarkers, cognitive testing, and wearable-derived physiological data into a longitudinal recovery measure that can be practically deployed in a resource-constrained setting such as Nigeria.

The proposed pilot therefore tests whether integrating multiple domains provides information beyond an existing, simpler physiological signal rather than assuming that a composite is automatically superior.

Innovation

The Resilience Index combines three clinical/physiological measures — C-reactive protein (CRP), HbA1c, and heart-rate variability (HRV) — with two cognitive measures — Trail Making Test-B and Digit Span — into a composite score representing an individual's physiological and cognitive state.

The study introduces a resilience-deficit area metric, adapted from the resilience-triangle concept, to quantify the magnitude and duration of deviation from an individual's baseline during recovery.

CRP and HRV are expected to provide information about physiological disruption and recovery across the study window. HbA1c is a comparatively stable marker of average glycaemic exposure and is included as a reserve/baseline-capacity indicator rather than as an acute recovery marker.

The pilot explicitly tests whether the multidomain composite adds information beyond HRV alone. The measurement approach uses minimally invasive or field-deployable methods, including fingerstick point-of-care testing, wearable HRV, and brief cognitive assessments.

Research Question

Does a composite resilience index derived from clinical and cognitive measures track physiological and cognitive disruption and recovery following a real-world stressor, and is the resulting recovery deficit associated with subsequent performance and readiness-to-train?

Study Design

Prospective, single-arm observational pilot study. Participants will be assessed at four standardized touchpoints relative to their own athletic competition:

Baseline: approximately 5 days before the event

Acute: immediately or within hours after the event

Peak: approximately 24–48 hours after the event

Recovery check: approximately 7–10 days after the event

This repeated-measures design allows each participant to serve as their own baseline reference while evaluating the magnitude and trajectory of recovery.

Participants

The study will aim for approximately 100 completers, with 120–150 participants recruited to allow for anticipated dropout and incomplete follow-up.

Participants will be adults aged 18–40 who actively train or compete in soccer, running, volleyball, tennis, or skating and have an upcoming eligible sporting event.

Athletes are the initial proving ground because competition provides a naturally occurring, measurable stressor with observable performance outcomes. The pilot is not intended to establish universal validity across all populations.

Measurements

Clinical/physiological: CRP and HbA1c using fingerstick point-of-care testing; resting ln-rMSSD HRV using a chest-strap/wearable device.

Cognitive: Trail Making Test-B; Digit Span forward and backward.

Functional/performance: standardized sport-specific performance test.

Subjective: Short Recovery-Stress Scale (SRSS) as the primary subjective measure; RESTQ-Sport as a secondary measure at baseline and recovery.

Primary Outcome

The primary analysis will examine the association between each participant's resilience-deficit area — the trapezoidal area between their recovery trajectory and personal baseline — and their percentage change in standardized sport-specific performance.

The primary regression model will adjust for sport as a categorical covariate.

The Resilience Index will be evaluated using the prespecified 50/50 clinical-cognitive weighting, with 60/40 used as a sensitivity analysis. The composite will also be benchmarked against HRV alone to determine whether the multidomain measure provides meaningful additional information.

Expected Result and Falsification

The pilot is an empirical test, not a confirmation exercise.

If the composite demonstrates a meaningful and robust association with subsequent performance and adds information beyond HRV alone, this would provide preliminary evidence supporting multidomain recovery tracking.

If it does not, or if its apparent value disappears after appropriate adjustment and sensitivity analyses, that result will be treated as evidence against the current architecture and will guide revision.

Impact

The immediate goal is to make recovery capacity measurable and to establish whether the proposed measurement architecture is scientifically useful.

A positive proof-of-concept result would support larger, independently validated studies in additional populations, potentially including health workers and older adults. The pilot does not claim to predict longevity, future disease, or long-term health outcomes; those applications require separate prospective validation.
